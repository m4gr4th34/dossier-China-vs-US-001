# The US–China Century Ledger

*Irfan Ali Khan — Independent Researcher*

A century of US-China momentum in seven figures (with words in between, if you really want them).

This dossier is a two-country **achievement ledger** told in seven figures: a per-row-verified record of American and Chinese innovation, infrastructure, and social achievements from 1926 to 2026, built under a published selection rule and a published promotion standard. It is deliberately neither a triumphalist nor a declinist account — every row wears an honest status label, and the momentum layer is computed only as an explicitly **OPEN-CAVEATED** constructed index — shown with its full sensitivity bands and named exclusions, never as a measured verdict. The honest state today: 298 candidate rows, 294 ESTABLISHED, the rest one open challenge (OPEN-UNVERIFIED) and three provenance-flagged REPORTED items. The dossier now carries three layers, each labeled: the **record** (the ledger and figures), an **interpretation** of it (OPEN-CAVEATED, re-weightable), and dated **bets** (FORECASTs with falsifiable signposts). **Don't trust this paper — run it.**

**READING THE FIGURES** — the decoder, so the seven figures below stand on their own without any prose. **US is blue, China is red.** Texture is verification status: solid = ESTABLISHED, outlined = OPEN, hatched = REPORTED. > **AUTHORIAL JUDGMENT · 2026-07-27** — **If you were a founder choosing where to build, the author's judgment is the United States — for most founders, most sectors, most of the time.** This is a *labeled authorial judgment*, not a caption fact and not a verified claim; it is conditioned on four legs, each tied to Figures Va–Vb. **(1) Nationality and openness.** China's founder ecosystem is effectively closed to non-Chinese founders and, since 2020, constrained even for its own (the Va regime band; the VC collapse from $146B to $38B in the Vb strip); the US ecosystem remains open, including to immigrant founders. **(2) Sector.** In *bits* — software, internet, frontier AI — the US leads decisively (the recent US founding cluster and GPT-5, `US-2025-1`); in *atoms* — hardware, EVs, batteries, solar — the honest answer is a hybrid, a US cap table riding a Chinese supply chain (China's manufacturing and battery dominance, `CN-2010-4`, `CN-2017-2`). Figure Vc makes this split explicit as a *velocity* difference: China moves faster in atoms (deployment, hardware iteration, cutthroat selection), the US at the frontier and in bits. **(3) Regime risk.** The 2020–21 crackdown, the venture-capital collapse, and high-profile founder retreat are a standing risk a founder must price in — each leg is the Va band annotation and the committed Vb VC series, not rhetoric. **(4) The capital that remains, and its conditions.** As private venture funding collapsed, Chinese state capital only partly replaced it, and asymmetrically: it is abundant for nationally-aligned hard tech — chips, AI, quantum (the ~$1.8T guidance-fund target and Big Fund III's $47.5B, `CN-2024-3`, on the Vb state-capital band) — but scarce or closed for the misaligned, the foreign, and the consumer-facing, so a founder's access turns on whether the state reads their sector as strategic (the Vb state-capital band and `notes/founder_series_selection.md`). **An amendment, from the author's own experience (2026-07-28).** The recommendation above holds on the century-scale evidence — regime continuity and capital depth — but it is *era-conditioned*, and it is not what I lived. As a hard-tech founder in the roughly 2005–2020 window I watched the US venue *systematically underfund long-ROI atoms*: biotech diagnostics, quantum key distribution, and digital mental health were all things I could not fund in the US on any reasonable terms — while China was funding the QKD stack all the way to orbit (Micius, the first quantum-communications satellite, `CN-2016-1`). I am stating a further judgment plainly and as my own, not as a measured claim: the US early-stage *selection mechanism* in that era rewarded warm-intro network access and near-term software multiples over deep-tech potential — a gatekeeping filter on who gets seen, not on who is right. The CHIPS-era pivot to hard tech is real but *reactive and unproven*: a policy response to losing the atoms race, not evidence the private US venue has repaired its own selection. Falsifiable: if by 2030 US early-stage deep-tech funding-per-founder (fusion, quantum, advanced manufacturing, longevity) visibly closes on China's state-directed levels and the warm-intro filter measurably loosens, retire this amendment. A reader who weighs openness, sector, risk, and the terms of that capital differently can reach a different judgment; the figures give them the same evidence to do so.

## 01 THE QUESTION

Whether American or Chinese momentum (Here 'momentum' means the relative rate at which a country accumulates notable, dateable achievements over time — not GDP, not military power, and not a prediction. In this dossier it is a constructed index computed over the achievement ledger and published as OPEN-CAVEATED: every output states its rubric and shows how it moves as the weighting changes.) is ahead is one of the most argued questions in geopolitics, and it is argued mostly with anecdotes. Serious scholarship splits into two poles: one holds the American lead durable [Beckley '18], the other reads the trajectory as a classic rising-power challenge [Allison '17]. That the field is genuinely divided is itself the only safe thing to assert up front — both narratives are cottage industries, and each can curate a list of achievements to "win".

This dossier's bet is that the useful thing to publish is not another verdict but the instrument underneath one: a single, auditable ledger of what each country actually did and when, with every entry labeled to say exactly how well-checked it is. Build the ledger honestly first; argue about the scoreboard second — and only with the scoreboard's rubric fully exposed. Nothing on this page hands you a single winner: the momentum index (Figure II above) IS computed, but only as an openly constructed, re-weightable index that shows the answer changing with the rubric — a scoreboard you can audit and overturn, not a verdict.

*Map the achievements before scoring them — and never let the scoreboard smuggle itself in as a fact.*

## 02 WHAT'S ESTABLISHED — THE CORPUS AND ITS DISCIPLINES

The corpus is 298 candidate rows spanning 1926–2026, split between the US and China. Each row is **event-anchored** — tied to one specific dateable event (a founding, a first flight or launch, a completion, a commercial deployment, a discovery, a milestone) rather than a vague "era". A row becomes ESTABLISHED only when it clears a published promotion standard, held in `notes/verification_ritual.md`: a precise falsifiable claim; at least one qualifying independent source (or two independent journalistic/encyclopedic ones); its date confirmed against that specific anchor; and any conflicting US/Chinese figures recorded rather than silently adjudicated. 294 rows have cleared that bar; the full 1926–2025 chronological corpus has been processed, and per-row author-proposed passes (batch 11 physical-manufacturing / 2023–2025 achievements; batch 12 maritime and defense-industrial; batch 13 the national-security view; batches 14–15 founder-history and state-capital / frontier-AI rows) then added rows on both sides — each through the same ritual, not waved in for being proposed.

The selection rule, in `notes/selection_criteria.md`, is country-neutral by construction: a category is assigned by a claim's primary significance, not by which country produced it, so the same test that files a US firm's founding files a Chinese one. Three disciplines guard the sensitive cases. The **event/magnitude split**: a reform's occurrence can be ESTABLISHED while its contested output figure is caveated — China's reform-era poverty reduction, for instance, is asserted on the World Bank's independent series [World Bank], with China's own poverty-line count noted alongside rather than blended in. **Data-integrity**: where US and Chinese figures for one quantity conflict, both are shown. And a **trajectory rule**: a multi-year cumulative achievement may stand as a single row only under an independently-maintained series, an explicit span written into the claim, and filing at the span's start year — so a mid-span marker can never masquerade as a discrete event.

The shape of the corpus is itself a set of ESTABLISHED facts — but facts **about the ledger under this selection rule**, not a verdict about the world. Under the rule, US coverage is comparatively steady across the decades and weighted toward invention and commercial-deployment anchors; Chinese coverage is thin in the early decades, rises in the later ones, and is weighted toward completion and milestone anchors. Both patterns are partly real and partly artifacts — of era, of what each category happens to reward, and of English-language source availability during drafting. They describe how the ledger is populated. They are emphatically **not** a momentum score, and nothing here should be read as one.

The corpus is easier to grasp as a picture than a table — which is why **Figure I above** is the Century Spine, one block per row at its anchor year, the US stacking up from the centreline and China down, coloured by category and textured by verification label (solid ESTABLISHED, outlined OPEN, hatched REPORTED). It is a raw-count portrait of the ledger under the selection rule, not a momentum score; **click any year** to open that year's cards in place, or follow a block's deep-link into its year dossier on the audit-trail edition (`dossier.html#y-YYYY`). Beneath the blocks, a thin **volume strip** answers the obvious objection: that block canon is *flat by construction* (the selection rule targets a handful of rows per country per decade), so it deliberately cannot show the century's actual scale-up — the strip does, plotting measured R&D expenditure on a log axis. The contrast between the two is the point.

**Go deeper: the disciplines in one place**

The promotion standard, the fail outcomes, and the labels all live in versioned notes. When a candidate row fails the standard it does not vanish: it stays OPEN-UNVERIFIED (with the reason logged), or is reclassified **REPORTED** with the interested party named, or is removed to a logged tombstone in `notes/removed_rows.md` — never silently deleted. REPORTED is reserved for an interested-party magnitude claim that independent work has not reproduced; exactly one row carries it today (a 1952 output-recovery figure resting on contested official statistics), and it gets a vote on nothing. The trajectory rule is the newest discipline, applied country-blind: a US wartime-production row and a US shale row were reshaped by the very same rule that reshaped a Chinese poverty row and a Chinese expressway row.

### Velocity — four ways to be fast

Figures Va and Vb measure the *environment* a founder operates in — capital, exits, the regime. Every underlying figure is real and sourced (IEA, AlixPartners, McKinsey, Epoch AI, the UK AI Security Institute, US NIST/CAISI), but the choice of four dimensions and the US-vs-China pairing are the author's; re-weight them and the picture moves. The finding is not one-directional and that is the point: **China leads velocity in *atoms*** — electric-vehicle adoption ran from ~6% to ~45% of new-car sales in four years while the US stalled near 10%; China adds more solar in a year than the US has cumulatively and runs ~48,000 km of high-speed rail to America's ~0; its car-development cycles are roughly twice as fast; and its markets select with a ferocity (487 EV brands in 2018 heading for ~15 survivors) that forges champions *and* destroys capital. But **the US leads velocity in *bits* and at the frontier** — it produced the fastest-scaling consumer product in history, spends on AI infrastructure at a scale two orders of magnitude beyond China's, sets the AI capability frontier that Chinese open models trail by four to eight months, and now hosts the fastest-scaling startups of all. ## 04 WHAT WE MAKE OF IT

Interpretation, not record — every claim below cites its own rows or figures; re-weight them and disagree.

> **OPEN-CAVEATED** — **This is the one section where the dossier interprets rather than records.** The six numbered claims are the author's reading of the ledger and the figures — offered as OPEN-CAVEATED, true only within the frame stated, with the underlying rows and figures cited inline so a hostile reader can re-weight them and reach a different reading. The record is upstairs (the ledger and Figures I–Vc); this is what we make of it. It supersedes the previously walled candidate note (`notes/synthesis_frame_CANDIDATE.md`), now kept only as provenance history.

**i. The century bifurcated by domain, not by winner.** The recent ledger does not show one country pulling ahead so much as the two sorting onto opposite ends of a physical-stack-to-frontier-stack axis. China's recent ESTABLISHED rows cluster on the physical economy — solar-manufacturing dominance (`CN-2010-4`), the world's largest industrial-robot market (`CN-2013-3`), battery leadership (`CN-2017-2`, `CN-2020-4`, `CN-2025-2`), rare-earth processing (`CN-2024-2`) — the same shape the Figure IV manufacturing and trade strips carry and the Figure Vc deployment and cutthroat panels (① and ④) amplify. The US recent rows cluster on the frontier — frontier AI (`US-2025-1`), AI-hardware design leadership (`US-2024-2`), fusion science (`US-2024-4`), reusable spaceflight (`US-2025-2`) — matching the Figure Vc frontier-lag panel (②). Bits, bio and space versus the physical economy: ahead at different things, not one ahead overall.

**ii. Two systems optimized for different things.** The event-type signature in the ledger (the US invention- and deployment-heavy; China completion- and milestone-heavy — the Coverage-shape avenue) and Figures Va–Vb read as two machines tuned for different outputs: a private-capital system that converts frontier science into companies (Figure Vb's venture and exits strips), and a state-priority system that deploys chosen technologies at tempo (Figure Vc panel ①). Neither is strictly better; they are good at different halves of innovation.

**iii. The founder's answer is conditional.** On the evidence of Figure Vb and its judgment box, the United States is the default choice for most founders and most sectors — regime continuity (open throughout the century), capital depth, and openness to immigrant founders. The sharp exception is nationally-aligned hard tech: under state capital (the Figure Vb state-capital band and `notes/founder_series_selection.md`), funding is abundant if a founder's sector is read as strategic and closed if it is foreign, consumer, or politically misaligned. The answer is not "US" or "China" but "the US, unless you are building aligned hard tech."

**iv. The open experiment.** The one thing the record cannot yet settle: whether nationalized venture capital — the state substituting for the private venture market that collapsed after 2020 (Figure Vb) — can sustain FRONTIER innovation, not merely deployment, over a full decade. No prior case resolves it. Because it hinges on facts not yet in evidence, it is a forecast, not a finding — see §05.

**v. What would change our minds — the falsification menu.** This reading is not idle: four observations would move it. An independently-verified Chinese EUV-class lithography capability would promote the currently-REPORTED claim (`CN-2025-3`) and show the physical stack reaching frontier fabrication. US deployment tempo visibly closing the atoms gap (Figure Vc panel ①) would break claim (i). Chinese private venture capital recovering past half its 2021 peak (Figure Vb) would break claim (iii) and rewrite the judgment box. And a US industrial-policy row set matching China's completion tempo would collapse claim (ii)'s "two machines" framing. Each is a concrete, dated thing we are watching — which is exactly what the forecasts in §05 make explicit.

**vi. Leverage is mutual, and it splits by domain.** Neither side simply depends on the other; each holds a chokepoint the other is spending to escape. **China's chokepoints are atoms** — rare-earth processing (`CN-2024-2`), battery cells and materials (`CN-2017-2`), and sheer manufacturing capacity (`CN-2010-4`) — inputs the rest of the world cannot quickly re-source. **The US's are bits and tools** — semiconductor export controls, EUV-lithography access, frontier compute, and the listing venues themselves (the post-2021 US-listing freeze on the Figure Vb IPO strip). The proof that the leverage bites is the spending to break it: China stood up Big Fund III's $47.5B (`CN-2024-3`) precisely because the US chip-tool chokepoint hurt, and is chasing domestic EUV (the REPORTED `CN-2025-3`); the US is reshoring leading-edge fabrication (TSMC Arizona, `US-2024-3`) precisely because the atoms chokepoint hurt. Both sides are paying to escape the other's grip, and the payments are the measurement. Trend — stated as an **OPEN-CAVEATED direction, not a fact**: atoms-leverage erodes slower than bits-leverage, because a refining complex or a leading-edge fab takes many years and enormous capital to replicate while a model or a tool restriction can be routed around or leapfrogged faster — so on current evidence China's atoms chokepoints look stickier than the US's bits ones. That is a direction the next decade will settle, not a measurement.
