## The observation

The batch-11 tail-and-stack rows, laid beside the existing corpus, make a pattern
*visible* (they do not prove it): the two countries' recent achievements cluster on
opposite ends of a possible **physical-stack ↔ frontier-stack** axis.

- **China's recent ESTABLISHED rows concentrate on the physical/production stack** — solar-PV
  manufacturing capacity (CN-2010-4), rare-earth processing (CN-2024-2), industrial-robot
  installation dominance (CN-2013-3), battery deployment and market leadership (CN-2020-4,
  CN-2017-2, CN-2025-2). The "atoms" layer: making things at scale.
- **US recent ESTABLISHED rows concentrate on the frontier/design/science stack** — frontier
  AI models (GPT-5 US-2025-1, and ChatGPT earlier), AI-hardware design leadership (Nvidia
  US-2024-2), fusion science (US-2024-4), reusable-spaceflight firsts (US-2025-2). The
  "bits + frontier science" layer: inventing the next thing.
- The **crossing case is the interesting one**: leading-edge chip *fabrication* is now
  (partly) reshoring to US soil (TSMC Arizona, US-2024-3), and the EUV-lithography claim
  China is *reported* to chase (CN-2025-3, REPORTED) is precisely an attempt to move up the
  physical stack into frontier fabrication. The axis is contested exactly where each side is
  trying to enter the other's half.

## Why it is only a candidate

The pattern is at real risk of being an **artifact of the selection and of the era**, not a
fact about the world — the exact trap the dossier's whole discipline exists to catch:

1. **Selection artifact.** The rows were chosen under a rulebook; a bifurcation visible in a
   ~5–15-rows-per-decade curated canon may be a property of what got curated, not of the
   economies. (See `selection_criteria.md` §8 biases.)
2. **Category coupling.** The `industrial` vs `innovation`/`science` category split *already*
   encodes something close to this axis, so "China is industrial-heavy, US innovation-heavy"
   risks being true *by construction* — a restatement of the coverage-shape avenue, not a new
   finding.
3. **Recency window.** The bifurcation is loudest in the last ~15 years; the earlier corpus
   does not obviously carry it.

## What it would be *for* (if it survived scrutiny)

- **Index layer:** a candidate *second axis* for the momentum construction — instead of (or
  beside) a single "who leads" bar, a two-dimensional read (physical-stack momentum vs
  frontier-stack momentum), which could dissolve some of the "who's ahead" ambiguity into
  "ahead at what." Would stay OPEN-CAVEATED like the rest of the index; the axis definition
  would itself be a published, challengeable authorial construction.
- **Projections rung:** the bifurcation suggests distinct *rate-limiting factors* per side
  (China's physical stack exposed to demographics, energy, and trade access; the US frontier
  stack exposed to talent, capital concentration, and its dependence on others' physical
  stack). Any projection using this frame stays EXPLORATORY-CONJECTURE and must name its
  premise, a distinguishing signature, and its cost — per the constitution.

## What would confirm or refute it (before it earns any reader-facing use)

- **Refute:** a category-blind re-scoring (mask `category`) that makes the bifurcation
  vanish → it was category coupling. Or: filling the earlier decades shows the same split →
  it is not a recent phenomenon and the "momentum shift" reading is wrong.
- **Confirm-enough-to-use:** the split survives a category-neutral operationalization (e.g.
  an independent "position in the value chain" coding done blind to country), AND holds on an
  out-of-sample set of achievements not used to notice it.

Until then: this stays here, in a walled candidate-frame note, and is cited by nothing.

## Appendix (batch 12) — military-shipbuilding cost-effectiveness: a walled data point, NOT a row

**Same wall as above: NOT ASSERTED ANYWHERE READER-FACING; cited by nothing; not a ledger
row.** Recorded here only so the observation is not lost, with its provenance stated plainly.

The maritime pass surfaced a comparative claim that is deliberately kept OUT of the ledger:
the "China builds more warships, and far more cheaply" cost-effectiveness thesis, usually
attached to a widely-circulated figure that **China's shipbuilding CAPACITY is ~200× that of
the United States.**

**Provenance of the ~200× figure (why it is not a row):**
- The number traces to a **US Office of Naval Intelligence (ONI) briefing slide** — China
  shipyard capacity ~**23.2 million tons** vs US **<100,000 tons** (~232×; "200×" is the
  rounded popular version). The slide also projected ~475 PLAN battle-force ships by 2035.
- It reached the public as a **leaked slide** reported by *The War Zone* (Trevithick,
  11 Jul 2023), then amplified (Fox News, Alliance for American Manufacturing at "232×").
  The US Navy **authenticated** the slide but said it was "developed... from multiple public
  sources" and was "**not intended as a deep-dive into the PRC's commercial shipbuilding
  industry**."
- **Source class: interested-party (ONI / US Navy intelligence) + leaked + journalistic
  surfacing.** Not peer-reviewed, not a formally published ONI dataset, methodology opaque.
- **Substantive caveat:** the 23.2 M-ton figure is *total commercial shipyard tonnage*, which
  conflates commercial output with *naval* output — it is not a like-for-like warship-cost or
  warship-capacity ratio. The directional claim ("China out-produces the US in naval hulls,
  at lower cost") is defensible and consistent with the PLA-Navy hull-count row (CN-2020-5);
  the specific **200×/232× number is a briefing-slide artifact** and must never be stated as a
  measured fact.

**If it ever earns reader-facing use:** only as an explicitly-attributed, provenance-flagged
data point ("a leaked-then-authenticated ONI slide put China's total shipyard capacity at
~230× the US's, a figure that conflates commercial with naval output"), never as a bare
ratio, and never as a premise for any higher-status claim. Until then it stays here, walled.

This coheres with the physical-stack/frontier-stack candidate frame above: China's naval
mass is a downstream expression of its commercial-shipbuilding (physical-stack) dominance
(CN-2010-5), while US naval strength is concentrated in high-end tonnage and per-ship
capability (carriers, nuclear submarines) — the same atoms-vs-frontier split, in the
maritime domain. Still a candidate lens, still asserted nowhere.
